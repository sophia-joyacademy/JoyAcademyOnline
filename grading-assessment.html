Grading & Assessment

How we measure learning — and why

Our Approach to Assessment

At Joy Academy Online, grades are designed to reflect more than content mastery. Every course blends measurable knowledge with our 5C school-wide learning outcomes — Communication, Collaboration, Critical Thinking, Creativity, and Confidence — and is built around increasing levels of cognitive rigor, guided by Bloom's Taxonomy. A grade should tell a student and a parent not just what a student knows, but how well they can think, reason, and grow.

Assessment Types

Every course uses a mix of four assessment types, each serving a different purpose in the learning cycle:

Participation

Engagement in live sessions, forums, and peer review — where communication and collaboration are practiced directly.

Performance Tasks

Real-world applications — projects, essays, and problem sets that ask students to use knowledge, not just recall it.

Formative

Quizzes, exit tickets, and discussion posts that check understanding along the way and inform instruction before a unit ends.

Summative

Unit tests, midterms, and finals that measure mastery of content at the end of a unit, quarter, or semester.

Grading Categories & Weights

Each semester's and each quarter's grade is made up of several weighted assessment categories. Different categories can carry different weights, and different subjects and teachers may choose the categories and weights that best fit their instruction. Below are the reference grading categories and reference weights, with the Midterm Exam counted in Q1 and the Final Exam in Q2:

Grading Category Sample Weight
Classroom Contribution (Communication, Collaboration, Confidence)10%
Language Skills (Vocabulary & Grammar)10%
Homework15%
Quiz15%
Unit Test25%
Midterm Exam (Q1) / Final Exam (Q2)25%
Total100%
Late Work: Individual teachers set the specific late-work and makeup policy in their course syllabus. As a general guideline, late work may be accepted with a modest deduction at the teacher's discretion, and makeup opportunities are available for excused absences.

How the 5Cs Show Up in Grading

The 5Cs are not a separate grading line — they are assessed within the categories above. Teachers build a rubric for these categories so each relevant skill is an explicit, visible criterion:

  • Classroom Contribution — Communication (discussion, verbal explanation), Collaboration (peer interaction, group work), Confidence (participation, willingness to engage)
  • Homework — Critical Thinking (problem-solving approach), Creativity (varied solution strategies)

Language Skills: Vocabulary & Grammar

This category measures a student's command of the foundational vocabulary and grammar of a subject — the competencies that let them engage effectively with that subject's material. A gap in these skills can get in the way of fully understanding the subject. What's measured varies by subject:

  • Math & Science — subject-specific vocabulary (terms, definitions) and the grammar needed to read and write technical language: parsing long or compound sentences, word problems, and precise scientific phrasing. Not a heavy writing load.
  • English & Social Science — vocabulary and grammar in the traditional sense: word usage, sentence construction, and written expression.

Homework: Practice & Application

Homework is designed to span multiple levels of Bloom's Taxonomy rather than sitting at a single level of difficulty. Most assignments are Practice/Skill Drills, building the foundational thinking skills of Remember and Understand; homework should also include a share of higher-order work — Apply and Analyze-level tasks — so students build toward deeper, independent thinking alongside the basics. Both tiers are graded within the single Homework category — teachers vary the point value per assignment based on its difficulty and thinking level, rather than splitting it into separate grading lines:

  • Practice / Skill Drills — guided practice, worked examples, and vocabulary exercises that reinforce a single skill.
  • Application Tasks — deeper, multi-step reasoning (e.g., word problems in Math) or extended writing (e.g., short essays or analytical responses in English and Social Studies).

Bloom's Taxonomy: Building Deeper Thinking

Bloom's Taxonomy is a framework for thinking skills used widely across education systems worldwide, describing how a student's thinking develops from simple recall toward more complex analysis, judgment, and creation. In the 21st century, the most important ability is no longer how much knowledge a student can remember, but whether they can apply, analyze, evaluate, and create — in other words, higher-order thinking. That's why Joy Academy Online deliberately designs its curriculum to shift instruction in this direction, training students' thinking to move from memorization toward deeper, independent thought.

Bloom's Taxonomy organizes thinking into six levels of increasing complexity. Joy Academy Online designs coursework so that students move beyond recall — Homework, Quizzes, and Tests/Exams are built to engage multiple levels rather than staying at the bottom of the taxonomy throughout the quarter.

The first two levels — Remember and Understand — are foundational thinking skills. The remaining four — Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create — are higher-order thinking skills that require students to transfer, question, judge, and generate, not just recall.

Remember

FoundationalKnowledge

Recall facts, terms, and basic concepts without necessarily understanding their meaning.

Sample Verbs

Define, List, Identify, Name, Recall, Label, Recognize, Retrieve

Sample Questions
  • Can you list three...?
  • Can you recall...?
  • Who was...? When did... happen?

Understand

FoundationalComprehension

Demonstrate basic understanding of facts and ideas by organizing, comparing, and interpreting information.

Sample Verbs

Explain, Summarize, Classify, Compare, Interpret, Paraphrase, Discuss

Sample Questions
  • Can you explain what is happening in...?
  • How would you compare...?
  • What is the main idea of...?

Apply

Higher-OrderApplication

Use acquired knowledge, facts, and rules in a new situation to solve problems in different ways.

Sample Verbs

Apply, Demonstrate, Solve, Use, Illustrate, Organize, Calculate

Sample Questions
  • How would you use... to solve...?
  • What examples can you find to...?
  • How would you organize... to show...?

Analyze

Higher-OrderAnalysis

Examine details closely — break down information, identify causes, and find evidence to support conclusions.

Sample Verbs

Analyze, Compare, Contrast, Examine, Categorize, Investigate, Distinguish

Sample Questions
  • What is the relationship between...?
  • What evidence can you find for...?
  • Can you distinguish between...?

Evaluate

Higher-OrderEvaluation

State and defend a position by judging the validity of information, ideas, or the quality of work against a set of criteria.

Sample Verbs

Judge, Justify, Critique, Defend, Assess, Prioritize, Recommend

Sample Questions
  • Do you agree with...? Why or why not?
  • How would you prioritize...?
  • What data was used to reach this conclusion?

Create

Higher-OrderSynthesis

Compile information in a new way, or propose an original solution, by combining ideas into a new pattern.

Sample Verbs

Design, Construct, Develop, Formulate, Invent, Compose, Propose

Sample Questions
  • What changes would you make to solve...?
  • Can you design an original...?
  • Can you propose an alternative to...?

How Coursework Aligns to Bloom's Levels

The core of Anderson and Krathwohl's (2001) revised framework is a two-dimensional "Taxonomy Table": the vertical axis is the Knowledge Dimension, the horizontal axis is the Cognitive Process Dimension — together, they can locate any learning objective, classroom activity, or assessment task within the overall map of thinking development. Here is the basic structure of that table:

Knowledge Dimension 1. Remember 2. Understand 3. Apply 4. Analyze 5. Evaluate 6. Create
Recognizing, Recalling Interpreting, Exemplifying, Classifying, Summarizing, Inferring, Comparing, Explaining Executing, Implementing Differentiating, Organizing, Attributing Checking, Critiquing Generating, Planning, Producing
A. Factual Knowledge
B. Conceptual Knowledge
C. Procedural Knowledge
D. Metacognitive Knowledge
See the full breakdown of the Knowledge Dimension →
  • A. Factual Knowledge — The basic elements students must know to be acquainted with a discipline or solve problems in it.
    • Aa. Knowledge of terminology
    • Ab. Knowledge of specific details and elements
  • B. Conceptual Knowledge — The interrelationships among the basic elements within a larger structure that enable them to function together.
    • Ba. Knowledge of classifications and categories
    • Bb. Knowledge of principles and generalizations
    • Bc. Knowledge of theories, models, and structures
  • C. Procedural Knowledge — How to do something: methods of inquiry, and criteria for using skills, algorithms, techniques, and methods.
    • Ca. Knowledge of subject-specific skills and algorithms
    • Cb. Knowledge of subject-specific techniques and methods
    • Cc. Knowledge of criteria for determining when to use appropriate procedures
  • D. Metacognitive Knowledge — Knowledge of cognition in general, as well as awareness and knowledge of one's own cognition.
    • Da. Strategic knowledge
    • Db. Knowledge about cognitive tasks, including appropriate contextual and conditional knowledge
    • Dc. Self-knowledge
